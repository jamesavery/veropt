Bayesian Optimisation of Very Expensive Objectives}

\author{James Avery\\
  \small Department of Computer Science, University of Copenhagen\\
  \small \texttt{avery@diku.dk}}

\date{July 2026 --- working draft}

\begin{abstract}
Bayesian optimisation (BO) of very expensive objective functions --- e.g.\
coupled-cluster electronic structure energies that take months per evaluation
--- typically starts from an uninformative Gaussian process (GP) prior and
must spend a large fraction of its small evaluation budget rediscovering the
gross structure of the objective. In many such applications a fast surrogate
of the objective exists (harmonic approximations in microseconds,
semi-empirical methods in seconds) together with a \emph{known pointwise bound}
on its error: the true objective deviates from the proxy by at most a known
percentage, even though the locations of their optima may differ. We show how
to encode this knowledge exactly where it belongs in the BO machinery: the GP
prior mean is set to the proxy, and the prior covariance is scaled
heteroscedastically so that the prior standard deviation at every point is the
promised deviation band. The bound is treated as a hard upper limit on the
prior amplitude --- a single trainable scale factor may tighten the band as
data accumulates but is constrained never to exceed it. The construction
composes with arbitrary stationary correlation kernels and survives the data
renormalisation used in practical BO implementations. We document the
implementation in the open-source package \veropt{} down to the level of the
code that realises each equation, give a user guide with a complete,
runnable example, and benchmark the method: on the six-dimensional Hartmann
function with a proxy distorted by at most 1\%, the proxy-informed prior comes
within $0.2\%$ of the global optimum after a single Bayesian batch (12
evaluations) in every repetition, whereas a flat prior is still $18\%$ short
of the optimum when the budget of 24 evaluations is exhausted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bayesian optimisation is the method of choice for optimising black-box
functions whose evaluations are so expensive that only a small number ---
often around one hundred --- can be afforded
\citep{shahriari2016, frazier2018}. A Gaussian process surrogate is fitted to
the evaluations made so far, and an acquisition function balances exploration
against exploitation to select the next batch of points.

The standard GP prior is maximally uninformative: a constant mean and a
stationary kernel with a single global amplitude. Every evaluation must then
contribute to learning both the gross structure of the objective \emph{and}
the fine structure near the optimum. For the most expensive objectives this is
wasteful, because the gross structure is often already known. Our motivating
example is molecular electronic structure: a CCSD(T)-level energy surface may
cost months of computation per point, while a harmonic approximation to the
same surface is available in microseconds and semi-empirical electronic
structure methods in seconds. Crucially, these cheap surrogates frequently
come with \emph{quantitative} error guarantees of the form ``the true energy
is within $1\%$ of the approximation, everywhere''. The minima of the cheap
and the expensive surface need not coincide --- otherwise the problem would be
solved --- but pointwise, the expensive function is trapped in a known band
around the cheap one.

This paper describes a simple, well-founded way to inject exactly this kind of
knowledge into BO: build the GP prior from the proxy. The prior mean is the
proxy itself, and the prior covariance is rescaled so that the prior standard
deviation at each point equals the promised band. The result is that the
surrogate starts out already knowing everything the proxy knows, and the
expensive evaluations are spent exclusively on learning the \emph{residual}
between truth and proxy --- a function whose amplitude is bounded by the
guarantee. Related ideas appear in multi-fidelity BO
\citep{kennedy2000, forrester2007}, where cheap and expensive evaluations are
modelled jointly, and more broadly in grey-box BO \citep{astudillo2021},
which exploits known structure of the objective. The variant presented here
is deliberately minimal: it requires no evaluations of the expensive function
to fit the relationship between fidelities --- the auto-regressive coefficient
and discrepancy process of \citet{kennedy2000} are replaced by an identity
coupling and an amplitude bound, both supplied a priori by the user's
guarantee. This matters precisely in the regime we target: with months per
evaluation, there is no budget to learn the cross-fidelity relationship
empirically.

The paper is organised so that a reader can go from the mathematics to a
working optimisation of their own problem. Section~\ref{sec:method} develops
the construction and the two subtleties that make it work in practice
(kernel-diagonal calibration and behaviour under data whitening).
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} documents how the construction is realised
in the open-source package \veropt{} \citep{veropt}, quoting the code that
implements each equation and describing every change made to the existing
optimiser core. Section~\ref{sec:usage} is a user guide: the contract a proxy
must satisfy, the configuration options, a complete runnable example, and a
recipe for adapting it to a new problem. Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
benchmarks the method.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting}

Let $f \colon X \subset \real^d \to \real$ be the expensive objective, to be
maximised under a budget of $N \sim 10^2$ evaluations. (\veropt{} always
maximises; an objective to be minimised, such as an energy, is negated ---
nothing in the construction depends on the sign convention.) Let
$g \colon X \to \real$ be a fast proxy ($\mu$s--s per evaluation), and assume
the pointwise guarantee
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bound}
  \lvert f(x) - g(x) \rvert \;\le\; \varepsilon(x)
  \qquad \text{for all } x \in X,
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon$ known. The two cases of practical interest are the
\emph{relative} bound $\varepsilon(x) = r\,\lvert g(x)\rvert$ (``$f$ is within
$1\%$ of $g$'', $r = 0.01$) and the \emph{absolute} bound
$\varepsilon(x) = \varepsilon_0$ (``$f$ is within $1$~kcal/mol of $g$'').
Nothing is assumed about the locations of the optima of $f$ and $g$.

\subsection{The proxy-informed prior}

We place on $f$ the Gaussian process prior
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:prior}
  f \;\sim\; \mathcal{GP}\bigl(m, \, k\bigr),
  \qquad
  m(x) = g(x),
  \qquad
  k(x, x') = c^2\, \sigma(x)\, \sigma(x')\, \rho(x, x'),
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is a correlation kernel ($\rho(x,x) = 1$) and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sigma}
  \sigma(x) \;=\; \frac{\varepsilon(x)}{\kappa},
\end{equation}
with $\kappa > 0$ a confidence parameter discussed below, and $c \in (0, 1]$
a global amplitude factor discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cap}. The kernel in
\eqref{eq:prior} is the standard non-stationary scaling of a correlation
kernel by an outer product of positive functions, and is a valid covariance:
for any finite set of points $\{x_i\}$ the Gram matrix factors as
$K = D R D$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(c\,\sigma(x_i)\bigr)$ and $R$
the (positive semi-definite) correlation Gram matrix, and congruence by a
diagonal matrix preserves positive semi-definiteness.

In practice $\rho$ is obtained from an
arbitrary base kernel $k_{\mathrm{b}}$ by normalising with its diagonal,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:corr}
  \rho(x, x') \;=\;
  \frac{k_{\mathrm{b}}(x, x')}{\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}(x, x)\,k_{\mathrm{b}}(x', x')}},
\end{equation}
so that the construction stays calibrated even for base kernels with
$k_{\mathrm{b}}(x,x) \neq 1$ --- kernel sums and spectral mixtures being the
common offenders: a sum of two Mat\'ern kernels has $k_{\mathrm{b}}(x,x) = 2$
and would otherwise silently inflate the band by $\sqrt{2}$ past the promised
bound. Because $\rho(x,x) = 1$ exactly by \eqref{eq:corr}, the prior variance
at any point is $c^2 \sigma(x)^2$ by construction --- there is no additional
global outputscale to interfere with the calibration. The hyperparameters of
$k_{\mathrm{b}}$ (e.g.\ ARD lengthscales) remain free and are trained as
usual; they control the \emph{correlation structure} of the residual, while
its \emph{amplitude} is fixed by the guarantee.

Equation~\eqref{eq:prior} is most easily read through the residual
$h = f - g$: the prior states $h \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k)$ with pointwise
standard deviation $c\,\sigma(x)$, i.e.\ the model believes the truth deviates
from the proxy by a smooth function whose typical size at $x$ is a known
fraction of the band $\varepsilon(x)$. With $c = 1$ and $\kappa = 2$ the prior
places ${\sim}95\%$ of its mass inside the band \eqref{eq:bound} at every
point; $\kappa = 3$ gives ${\sim}99.7\%$. The bound is thus encoded
\emph{softly} --- a GP cannot represent a hard truncation --- and $\kappa$
expresses how literally the guarantee is taken. We default to $\kappa = 2$.

A worked number makes the calibration concrete. Suppose the objective is a
negated total energy and the proxy predicts $g(x) = -50.0$ energy units at
some point $x$, with a relative guarantee of $1\%$ ($r = 0.01$). Then
$\varepsilon(x) = 0.01 \times 50.0 = 0.5$, and with $\kappa = 2$ the prior at
$x$ is $f(x) \sim \mathcal{N}(-50.0,\, 0.25^2)$: its central $\pm 2\sigma$
interval is $[-50.5, -49.5]$ --- exactly the promised band. Far from any
expensive evaluation the posterior returns to this prior, so the surrogate is
never more ignorant than the guarantee allows, anywhere. If training later
tightens the amplitude to $c = 0.8$ (Section~\ref{sec:cap}), the band at $x$
narrows to $\pm 0.4$.

\subsection{The bound as a hard cap on a trained amplitude}
\label{sec:cap}

The guarantee \eqref{eq:bound} is an \emph{upper} bound: the proxy may well be
better than its worst case. We therefore make the global amplitude factor $c$
in \eqref{eq:prior} a hyperparameter, trained alongside the kernel
lengthscales by type-II maximum likelihood, but constrained to
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cap}
  c \in (c_{\min}, \, 1],
\end{equation}
implemented by a sigmoidal reparametrisation (a \texttt{gpytorch}
\citep{gardner2018} interval constraint). The cap at $1$ guarantees that no
amount of hyperparameter optimisation can inflate the prior beyond the
promised band, while the marginal likelihood is free to \emph{tighten} the
band as the data reveal that the proxy is more accurate than worst-case ---
which immediately sharpens the posterior everywhere, not only near
evaluations. The floor $c_{\min}$ (default $0.1$) prevents overconfident
collapse from the handful of points available early on. Setting $c$ constant
at $1$ recovers the literal worst-case prior.

One practical guard is worth stating here because it shapes the user-facing
options: under a relative bound $\sigma(x) \to 0$ wherever $g(x) \to 0$,
which pins the GP to the proxy and can destabilise Cholesky factorisations. A
small floor $\varepsilon(x) \gets \max(\varepsilon(x),
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}})$ restores numerical safety; the user chooses
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}$ in the objective's own units.

\subsection{Interaction with data normalisation}
\label{sec:normalisation}

Practical BO implementations whiten their data, and this is where a naive
implementation of \eqref{eq:prior} silently breaks. In \veropt{}, inputs and
observed objective values are affinely normalised to zero mean and unit
variance per dimension,
\begin{equation}
  T_x(x) = (x - \mu_x) \oslash s_x,
  \qquad
  T_y(y) = (y - \mu_y) / s_y,
\end{equation}
(with $\oslash$ elementwise division), and --- crucially --- the normalisers
are \emph{refitted} as new evaluations arrive, by default at every step in
the multi-objective case. The GP model lives entirely in whitened
coordinates; the proxy is defined in physical units. The prior mean must
therefore be evaluated as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:meannorm}
  \tilde m(\tilde x) \;=\; T_y\bigl(g(T_x^{-1}(\tilde x))\bigr)
  \;=\; \frac{g(T_x^{-1}(\tilde x)) - \mu_y}{s_y},
\end{equation}
where tildes denote whitened quantities. The band, being the standard
deviation of a \emph{deviation} rather than a value, transforms through the
scale of $T_y$ only:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sigmanorm}
  \tilde\sigma(\tilde x) \;=\; \sigma(x) / s_y
  \;=\; T_y\bigl(\sigma(x)\bigr) - T_y(0);
\end{equation}
applying the full affine map here would be incorrect, silently shifting the
band by the normalisation offset $\mu_y / s_y$. The second identity in
\eqref{eq:sigmanorm} is how the implementation computes the scale-only
transform: it only ever holds the normaliser as an opaque callable, and
$T_y(\sigma) - T_y(0)$ extracts the scale without access to the normaliser's
parameters, for any affine $T_y$.

Because the normalisers change between refits, the mean and kernel cannot
capture $T_x, T_y$ once at construction time; the implementation instead
binds them by \emph{late-bound closures} that read the current normaliser at
call time (Section~\ref{sec:wiring}).

\subsection{Cost}

The proxy is evaluated at every posterior query, which during acquisition
optimisation (here: dual annealing with sequential proximity-punished batch
selection) means thousands of calls per suggested batch. Microsecond- to
millisecond-scale proxies are negligible; second-scale proxies (semi-empirical
methods) dominate the suggestion step and benefit from memoisation, which we
leave as future work. Against an objective costing days to months per
evaluation, even the un-memoised cost is irrelevant.

\subsection{The host: \veropt{}'s model stack}

To read the changes, four facts about the package suffice.
(i)~\code{BayesianOptimiser} (\code{optimiser.py}) drives the loop and holds
all data in physical (``real'') units; it owns the normalisers and refits
them as configured. (ii)~The surrogate is a \code{GPyTorchFullModel}
(\code{model.py}): a list of \emph{independent} single-objective GPs
(\code{GPyTorchSingleModel}), one per objective. Each single model holds a
\code{mean\_module} (by default \code{gpytorch.means.\allowbreak{}ConstantMean()}) and a
kernel; the six kernels offered by the package (Mat\'ern, double Mat\'ern,
rational quadratic, a rational-quadratic--Mat\'ern product, spectral mixture,
spectral delta) are subclasses configuring that pair. (iii)~Training builds a
fresh \code{ExactGP} around the current (whitened) data and runs Adam on the
exact marginal log-likelihood; the mean and kernel \emph{modules} persist
across steps, so hyperparameters carry over. (iv)~Everything the model sees
--- training data, acquisition queries --- is in whitened units.

The feature therefore decomposes into: a new self-contained module
supplying the mean and the kernel wrapper (\S\ref{sec:newmodule}); small
changes to the model classes to install them and keep serialisation working
(\S\ref{sec:wiring}); plumbing that hands the current normalisers to the
model at the right moment (\S\ref{sec:wiring}); and the user-facing
configuration surface (\S\ref{sec:api}). The acquisition functions, the
acquisition optimisers, the normalisers, the training loop, and the plotting
code are untouched.

\subsection{The new module: \code{proxy\_prior.py}}
\label{sec:newmodule}

\paragraph{The user contract: \code{ProxyMeanFunction}.} The user supplies
the proxy by subclassing \code{ProxyMeanFunction}, an abstract savable class
mirroring the package's objective classes: physical units in, physical units
out. The base class validates shapes and finiteness around the user's
\code{\_run} method --- input \code{[n\_points, n\_variables]}, output
\code{[n\_points]} --- so a defective proxy fails loudly at the boundary
rather than deep inside a Cholesky factorisation. A concrete subclass appears
in the example of Section~\ref{sec:example}.

\paragraph{The prior mean: \code{ProxyMean}.} A \code{gpytorch.means.Mean}
that implements \eqref{eq:meannorm}. It holds the user's proxy and two
initially unset normalisation closures, installed later by
\code{update\_normalisation\_functions}:

\begin{lstlisting}
class ProxyMean(gpytorch.means.Mean):

    def __init__(self, mean_function):
        super().__init__()
        self.mean_function = mean_function
        self._unnormaliser_variables = None   # x-tilde -> x
        self._normaliser_objectives = None    # y -> y-tilde

    def evaluate_proxy_real_units(self, variable_values):
        batch_shape = variable_values.shape[:-2]
        n_points = variable_values.shape[-2]
        n_variables = variable_values.shape[-1]

        flattened_values = variable_values.reshape(-1, n_variables)

        # The proxy is evaluated without gradients. This is safe with
        # the current derivative-free acquisition optimisers but would
        # silence gradients through the prior mean if a gradient-based
        # acquisition optimiser is ever added.
        with torch.no_grad():
            variable_values_real_units = \
                self._unnormaliser_variables(flattened_values)
            proxy_values = self.mean_function(
                variable_values=variable_values_real_units
            )

        return proxy_values.reshape(*batch_shape, n_points)

    def forward(self, x):
        proxy_values_real_units = \
            self.evaluate_proxy_real_units(variable_values=x)
        with torch.no_grad():
            return self._normaliser_objectives(proxy_values_real_units)
\end{lstlisting}

\code{forward} is \eqref{eq:meannorm} read left to right: unwhiten the query
points, evaluate the proxy, whiten the result. The reshape bookkeeping exists
because \code{botorch} evaluates means on batched inputs
\code{[..., n, d]} during acquisition optimisation, while the user contract
is a plain \code{[n, d]} matrix; the batch dimensions are flattened for the
proxy call and restored afterwards. The same class provides the scale-only
transform of \eqref{eq:sigmanorm}, used by the kernel below:

\begin{lstlisting}
    def scale_objectives_to_normalised(self, objective_values):
        # Transforms a *deviation* (rather than a value): applies only
        # the scale of the normalisation, not the shift.
        with torch.no_grad():
            transformed_values = \
                self._normaliser_objectives(objective_values)
            transformed_zeros = \
                self._normaliser_objectives(
                    torch.zeros_like(objective_values))
            return transformed_values - transformed_zeros
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{The prior covariance: \code{ProxyScaledKernel}.} A
\code{gpytorch.kernels.\allowbreak{}Kernel} that wraps \emph{any} of the package's
kernels as the base kernel $k_{\mathrm{b}}$ and implements \eqref{eq:prior}
with the diagonal normalisation \eqref{eq:corr} built in. Writing the kernel
as
\begin{equation*}
  k(x, x') \;=\;
  \underbrace{\frac{c\,\sigma(x)}{\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}(x,x)}}}_{a(x)}
  \;
  \underbrace{\frac{c\,\sigma(x')}{\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}(x',x')}}}_{a(x')}
  \; k_{\mathrm{b}}(x, x'),
\end{equation*}
the \code{forward} is an outer product of the vector $a$ with itself, times
the base kernel matrix:

\begin{lstlisting}
class ProxyScaledKernel(gpytorch.kernels.Kernel):

    has_lengthscale = False

    def forward(self, x1, x2, diag=False, **params):
        base_values = self._evaluate_base_kernel(
            x1, x2, diag=diag, **params)

        # Normalise the base kernel by its diagonal so it becomes a
        # correlation kernel [eq. (4)]. Some kernels (sums of kernels,
        # spectral mixtures) do not have k(x, x) = 1, which would
        # silently inflate the prior band beyond the promised bound.
        base_diagonal_1 = self._evaluate_base_kernel(
            x1, x1, diag=True, **params).clamp(min=1e-12).sqrt()
        base_diagonal_2 = self._evaluate_base_kernel(
            x2, x2, diag=True, **params).clamp(min=1e-12).sqrt()

        # a(x) = c * sigma(x) / sqrt(k_b(x, x))
        amplitude_1 = self._amplitude(variable_values=x1) \
            * self.amplitude_factor / base_diagonal_1
        amplitude_2 = self._amplitude(variable_values=x2) \
            * self.amplitude_factor / base_diagonal_2

        if diag:
            return amplitude_1 * amplitude_2 * base_values
        else:
            return amplitude_1.unsqueeze(-1) \
                * amplitude_2.unsqueeze(-2) * base_values
\end{lstlisting}

The whitened band $\tilde\sigma$ of \eqref{eq:sigma}/\eqref{eq:sigmanorm} is
computed by \code{\_amplitude}, which is where the relative/absolute bound
choice, the $\kappa$ division, and the numerical floor live:

\begin{lstlisting}
    def _amplitude(self, variable_values):
        if self.settings.bound_type == 'relative':
            proxy_values_real_units = \
                self.proxy_mean.evaluate_proxy_real_units(
                    variable_values=variable_values)
            band_real_units = (
                self.settings.bound_value
                * proxy_values_real_units.abs()
            ).clamp(min=self.settings.amplitude_floor)

        elif self.settings.bound_type == 'absolute':
            band_real_units = torch.full(
                size=variable_values.shape[:-1],
                fill_value=self.settings.bound_value)

        # sigma(x) = epsilon(x) / kappa          [eq. (3)]
        sigma_real_units = \
            band_real_units / self.settings.bound_in_n_sigmas

        # sigma-tilde = T_y(sigma) - T_y(0)      [eq. (7)]
        return self.proxy_mean.scale_objectives_to_normalised(
            objective_values=sigma_real_units)
\end{lstlisting}

Note that \code{\_amplitude} works entirely in physical units until the last
line --- the guarantee, the floor, and $\kappa$ all have physical meaning ---
and only then applies the scale-only whitening. \code{ProxyScaledKernel}
declares \code{has\_lengthscale = False}: it owns no lengthscale and no
outputscale of its own, so the only hyperparameters under the optimiser's
control are the base kernel's (reached through the child module) and the
amplitude factor $c$.

\paragraph{The amplitude cap.} The constraint \eqref{eq:cap} uses
\code{gpytorch}'s standard reparametrisation machinery: the raw parameter is
unconstrained, and an \code{Interval} constraint maps it through a scaled
sigmoid into $(c_{\min}, 1]$. Gradient training can therefore push $c$
towards the cap but never across it.

\begin{lstlisting}
        if settings.train_amplitude_factor:
            self.register_parameter(
                name='raw_amplitude_factor',
                parameter=torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(())))

            # The deviation bound is an upper bound: the amplitude may
            # shrink with training but can never exceed the bound.
            self.register_constraint(
                param_name='raw_amplitude_factor',
                constraint=Interval(
                    lower_bound=settings.amplitude_factor_lower_bound,
                    upper_bound=1.0))

            # The sigmoidal transform is infinite at the boundary, so
            # initialise just inside the cap.
            self.amplitude_factor = torch.tensor(0.99)
\end{lstlisting}

With \code{train\_amplitude\_factor} off, the parameter is never registered
and the \code{amplitude\_factor} property returns the constant $1$: the
literal worst-case prior. The initialisation at $0.99$ rather than $1.0$ is
forced by the reparametrisation: the inverse sigmoid is infinite at the
boundary.

\paragraph{Settings.} All options travel in a small dataclass,
\code{ProxyPriorSettings}, validated from the user-facing typed dictionary
(Table~\ref{tab:settings} in Section~\ref{sec:usage}); \code{ProxyPrior}
bundles the user's mean function with these settings and is the unit that the
model classes install and serialise.

\subsection{Wiring into the existing optimiser core}
\label{sec:wiring}

Four existing files were changed. The changes are small and local, which is
the point of the design: the prior is confined to the mean and kernel
modules, exactly where GP theory says it belongs.

\paragraph{Installing the prior (\code{model.py}).} A
\code{GPyTorchSingleModel} accepts a proxy prior only \emph{before} it has
seen data --- swapping the prior under a trained model would silently
invalidate its hyperparameters:

\begin{lstlisting}
    def set_proxy_prior(self, proxy_prior):
        assert self.model_with_data is None, \
            "The proxy prior must be set before the model is given data."

        self.proxy_prior = proxy_prior

        proxy_mean = ProxyMean(
            mean_function=proxy_prior.mean_function)

        self.mean_module = proxy_mean       # replaces ConstantMean
        self.kernel = ProxyScaledKernel(    # wraps the chosen kernel
            base_kernel=self.kernel,
            proxy_mean=proxy_mean,
            settings=proxy_prior.settings)
\end{lstlisting}

Because the base kernel is registered as a child module of the wrapper, its
hyperparameters remain visible to the training loop and appear in the state
dictionary under \code{covar\_module.base\_kernel.*} --- nothing else in the
training machinery needs to know the wrapper exists.

Three consequences did need attention. First, kernel-specific code
(lengthscale constraints and getters in \code{kernels.py}) reaches for
\code{model\_with\_data.covar\_module} expecting the base kernel; a new
property transparently unwraps:

\begin{lstlisting}
    @property
    def data_model_base_covar_module(self):
        covar_module = self.model_with_data.covar_module
        if isinstance(covar_module, ProxyScaledKernel):
            return covar_module.base_kernel
        else:
            return covar_module
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent and the six kernel classes were mechanically switched to it.
Second, \code{ProxyMean} --- unlike the \code{ConstantMean} it replaces ---
has no trainable parameters, and \code{torch.optim.Adam} raises on an empty
parameter group; the parameter groups handed to the optimiser are now
filtered through a helper, \code{filter\_out\_empty\_parameter\_groups}.
Third, serialisation: the saved state gains a \code{proxy\_prior} entry
(the user proxy's name and constructor state plus the settings), and
\code{from\_saved\_state} re-applies \code{set\_proxy\_prior} \emph{before}
loading the state dictionary, so that the saved keys
(\code{covar\_module.base\_kernel.*}, \code{raw\_amplitude\_factor}) find
their modules. Files saved before the feature existed load unchanged.

\paragraph{Handing over the normalisers (\code{optimiser.py},
\code{prediction.py}).} Section~\ref{sec:normalisation} requires the mean
and kernel to see the \emph{current} $T_x^{-1}$ and $T_y$, across refits.
The optimiser exposes them as closures over \code{self}, so a single
installation stays fresh forever:

\begin{lstlisting}
    def get_unnormaliser_function_variables(self):
        def unnormalise_variables(variable_values):
            if self.return_normalised_data:
                variable_values = self._normaliser_variables \
                    .inverse_transform(variable_values)
            return variable_values
        return unnormalise_variables
\end{lstlisting}

The closure reads \code{self.\_normaliser\_variables} at \emph{call} time,
so a normaliser refit is picked up automatically; and if normalisation is
disabled entirely (\code{normalise=False}), the guard makes the closure the
identity and the whole construction transparently operates in physical
units. The pair of closures is injected at the top of the optimiser's
predictor update --- before training, because training is the first thing
that evaluates the mean:

\begin{lstlisting}
    def _update_predictor(self, train=True):
        # Must happen before training so the proxy prior mean can map
        # between unit systems. The closures read the current
        # normalisers at call time, so they survive refits.
        self.predictor.update_model_normalisation_functions(
            unnormaliser_variables=
                self.get_unnormaliser_function_variables(),
            normaliser_objectives=
                self.get_normaliser_function_objectives())

        self.predictor.update_with_new_data(
            variable_values=self.evaluated_variable_values.tensor,
            objective_values=self.evaluated_objective_values.tensor,
            train=train)
        ...
\end{lstlisting}

\code{update\_model\_normalisation\_functions} is a no-op on the
\code{Predictor} base class and is forwarded by \code{BotorchPredictor} to
the model, which forwards it to any \code{ProxyMean} present --- models
without a proxy prior are untouched by the entire code path. In the
multi-objective case each single-objective GP receives an adapter that
restricts the $[\ldots, n_{\mathrm{obj}}]$ objective normaliser to its own
column (\code{make\_single\_column\_objective\_normaliser}), since each GP
whitens against its own objective's statistics.

\subsection{The user-facing API (\code{constructors.py})}
\label{sec:api}

The package's constructor, \code{bayesian\_optimiser(...)}, configures the
model through a typed dictionary; it gains two keys, \code{proxy\_prior} and
\code{proxy\_prior\_settings}. A single proxy is broadcast to all
objectives; a list supplies one entry per objective, with \code{None}
leaving that objective at the uninformative default. Validation is eager and
specific: a settings dictionary without a proxy, a list of the wrong length,
a proxy whose \code{n\_variables} disagrees with the objective's, or an
unknown settings key each fail at construction time with a message naming
the offending objective. The mechanism composes with everything else the
constructor does, because \code{set\_proxy\_prior} is applied to the
freshly built single-objective models before any data reaches them.

The implementation is exercised by 23 dedicated tests
(\code{tests/test\_proxy\_prior.py}) covering the calibration identity
$k(x,x) = c^2\tilde\sigma(x)^2$ for all six kernels (including the
non-correlation ones), the scale-only whitening, the amplitude cap under
training, multi-objective mixing, serialisation round-trips, and constructor
validation --- a useful specification of intended behaviour for readers
extending the feature.

\section{Using proxy-informed priors}
\label{sec:usage}

This section is the user guide: what a proxy must satisfy, what the options
mean, and a complete example to copy from.

\subsection{Writing a proxy}

The proxy is a subclass of \code{ProxyMeanFunction} (importable directly
from \code{veropt}) with three obligations:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A unique \code{name} class attribute.} It identifies the
    class in save files, exactly as for user-defined objectives.
  \item \textbf{A \code{\_run} method in physical units, vectorised over
    points.} Input is a \code{torch} tensor of shape
    \code{[n\_points, n\_variables]} holding real variable values; output is
    the proxy objective per point, shape \code{[n\_points]} (a column
    \code{[n\_points, 1]} is accepted and flattened). Since \veropt{}
    maximises, a proxy for an energy to be minimised returns the negated
    energy.
  \item \textbf{Tolerance of boundary round-off.} Whitening and unwhitening
    can hand the proxy points a floating-point epsilon outside the variable
    bounds; a proxy with a strictly validated domain should clamp its input
    (one line, visible in the example below).
\end{enumerate}

Speed matters: the proxy is called thousands of times per suggestion step
inside the acquisition optimiser, so a call (over a whole batch of points)
should cost well under a second. It is evaluated under \code{torch.no\_grad()},
so it may use non-differentiable operations freely.

\subsection{Configuring the optimiser}

The proxy and its guarantee enter through the model choice dictionary:

\begin{lstlisting}
from veropt import bayesian_optimiser

optimiser = bayesian_optimiser(
    n_initial_points=8,
    n_bayesian_points=32,
    n_evaluations_per_step=4,
    objective=expensive_objective,
    model={
        'kernels': 'matern',
        'proxy_prior': my_proxy,
        'proxy_prior_settings': {'bound_value': 0.01},
    },
    n_points_before_fitting=4,
)
\end{lstlisting}

Two of these lines deserve comment. \code{bound\_value} is the guarantee and
the only required setting: here, ``the true objective is within $1\%$ of the
proxy''. And \code{n\_points\_before\_fitting} is lowered to a single batch:
by default the model is first fitted only towards the end of the random
initial phase, which is the right economy for an uninformative prior but
wastes the head start an informative one provides --- with a proxy prior the
model is worth fitting (and its amplitude factor worth training) as soon as
the normalisers can be estimated. Table~\ref{tab:settings} lists all
settings.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\code{proxy\_prior\_settings} keys. ``Physical units'' means the
  objective's own units, before any normalisation.}
  \label{tab:settings}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llp{0.34\linewidth}}
    \toprule
    Key & Default & Meaning \\
    \midrule
    \code{bound\_value} & required &
      $r$ (relative) or $\varepsilon_0$, physical units (absolute) \\
    \code{bound\_type} & \code{'relative'} &
      reading of \code{bound\_value}: \code{'relative'} or \code{'absolute'} \\
    \code{bound\_in\_n\_sigmas} & \code{2.0} &
      $\kappa$ in \eqref{eq:sigma}: band $=\kappa$ prior std.\ deviations \\
    \code{train\_amplitude\_factor} & \code{True} &
      train $c$ by marginal likelihood; else $c \equiv 1$ \\
    \code{amplitude\_factor\_lower\_bound} & \code{0.1} &
      $c_{\min}$ in \eqref{eq:cap} \\
    \code{amplitude\_floor} & \code{0.0} &
      $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}}$, physical units; keeps $\sigma > 0$
      where $g \approx 0$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

For multi-objective problems, supply one proxy (or \code{None}) and one
settings dictionary (or \code{None}) per objective:

\begin{lstlisting}
    model={
        'kernels': 'matern',
        'proxy_prior': [energy_proxy, None],
        'proxy_prior_settings': [{'bound_value': 0.01}, None],
    },
\end{lstlisting}

Saving and loading work as for user-defined objectives:
\code{save\_to\_json} stores the proxy's name, constructor state and
settings, and \code{load\_optimiser\_from\_state} re-installs the prior ---
provided the proxy subclass has been imported in the loading process, which
is the same rule the package already imposes on objective classes.

\subsection{A complete example}
\label{sec:example}

The listing below is the complete, runnable script shipped as
\code{examples/\allowbreak{}example\_proxy\_informed\_prior.py}. The ``expensive''
objective is the six-dimensional Hartmann function (a standard multimodal BO
benchmark, maximised, $f^* = 3.3224$); the proxy is the same function with a
$1\%$ multiplicative sinusoidal distortion, so the relative guarantee
\code{bound\_value = 0.01} holds \emph{exactly} by construction while the
distortion shifts the local geometry --- the honest analogue of a
semi-empirical surface against a coupled-cluster one.

\begin{lstlisting}
from typing import Literal

import botorch
import torch

from veropt import ProxyMeanFunction, bayesian_optimiser
from veropt.optimiser.practice_objectives import Hartmann


class DistortedHartmannProxy(ProxyMeanFunction):

    name = 'distorted_hartmann_proxy'

    def __init__(self, n_variables: int) -> None:
        super().__init__(n_variables=n_variables)
        self.function = botorch.test_functions.Hartmann(
            dim=n_variables,
            negate=True
        )

    def _run(self, variable_values: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # The proxy can receive values a floating point rounding
        # error outside the bounds, so functions with a strictly
        # validated domain (like botorch's) should clamp
        variable_values = variable_values.clamp(0.0, 1.0)

        true_values = self.function(variable_values)

        return true_values * (
            1.0 + 0.01 * torch.sin(
                6.0 * torch.pi * variable_values[:, 0])
        )


n_variables: Literal[6] = 6
n_evaluations_per_step = 4

objective = Hartmann(n_variables=n_variables)

proxy = DistortedHartmannProxy(n_variables=n_variables)

optimiser = bayesian_optimiser(
    n_initial_points=8,
    n_bayesian_points=32,
    n_evaluations_per_step=n_evaluations_per_step,
    objective=objective,
    model={
        'kernels': 'matern',
        'proxy_prior': proxy,
        'proxy_prior_settings': {
            # the objective is within 1% of the proxy...
            'bound_value': 0.01,
            # ...read as two standard deviations of the prior
            'bound_in_n_sigmas': 2.0,
            # keeps the prior variance away from zero where
            # the proxy is near zero
            'amplitude_floor': 0.01
        }
    },
    n_points_before_fitting=n_evaluations_per_step
)

for step in range(4):
    optimiser.run_optimisation_step()

best_objective_value = float(
    optimiser.evaluated_objectives_real_units.max())

print(
    f"\nBest objective value found: {best_objective_value:.4f} "
    f"(the optimum is 3.3224)"
)
\end{lstlisting}

To run it from a checkout of the repository (Python $> 3.13$):

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
pip install .
python examples/example_proxy_informed_prior.py
\end{lstlisting}

The four optimisation steps evaluate $16$ points: two batches of $4$ random
initial points, then two model-suggested batches. The run recorded for this
paper took four minutes on a single CPU core (the cost is dominated by the
acquisition optimiser, not the GP) and finished with

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
Best objective value found: 3.3148 (the optimum is 3.3224)
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent a $0.2\%$ optimality gap after $16$ evaluations of the
``expensive'' function, reached with the first model-suggested batch rather
than gradually. Runs are not bit-reproducible (the acquisition optimiser
draws from an unseeded global random state), but this behaviour is stable
across repetitions; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} quantifies it over five
paired repetitions against a flat prior.

\subsection{Adapting the example to your own problem}

The distance from the example to a real application is deliberately short:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Wrap your fast model.} Replace
    \code{DistortedHartmannProxy.\_run} with your surrogate --- a harmonic
    energy, a semi-empirical method, a coarse-grid simulation --- returning
    the (negated, if minimising) objective per input row, in physical units.
  \item \textbf{State your guarantee honestly.} A percentage bound
    (``within $1\%$'') is \code{bound\_type='relative'} with
    \code{bound\_value=0.01}; a uniform error bar (``within
    $1$~kcal/mol'') is \code{bound\_type='absolute'} with
    \code{bound\_value} in the objective's units. The bound is a promise the
    optimiser will exploit: if the truth escapes the band, the model can
    become confidently wrong and the acquisition may never look where the
    truth is better than the proxy suggests. If any expensive evaluations
    exist already, check them against the bound before promising it.
  \item \textbf{Guard the zero crossings.} Under a relative bound, set
    \code{amplitude\_floor} to a small value in physical units if the
    objective changes sign or passes near zero anywhere in the domain.
  \item \textbf{Start fitting early.} Set
    \code{n\_points\_before\_fitting} equal to
    \code{n\_evaluations\_per\_step}.
  \item \textbf{Leave the rest alone.} Kernels, acquisition, batching and
    normalisation all compose with the prior unchanged; multi-objective
    problems take a list of proxies with \code{None} for uninformed
    objectives.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We benchmark the example configuration of Section~\ref{sec:example} at a
larger budget, against an identical optimiser with the default flat prior.
The objective is the six-dimensional Hartmann function (maximised, optimum
$f^* = 3.3224$) and the proxy is the multiplicative harmonic distortion
\begin{equation}
  g(x) \;=\; f(x)\,\bigl(1 + 0.01 \sin(6\pi x_1)\bigr),
\end{equation}
so that the relative bound \eqref{eq:bound} with $r = 0.01$ holds exactly,
while the distortion shifts the local geometry of the optimum. Both methods
use a Mat\'ern-5/2 kernel with ARD, batches of $4$, $8$ initial random points
followed by $16$ Bayesian-phase points (a total budget of $24$ evaluations),
and \veropt{}'s defaults otherwise. The proxy-informed runs use $\kappa = 2$,
a trainable amplitude $c \in (0.1, 1]$, and an absolute band floor of
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{floor}} = 0.01$. Each configuration is repeated five
times with paired initial points (the torch seed is shared between arms; the
acquisition optimiser retains its own stochasticity).

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} and Table~\ref{tab:results} report the best
objective value found as a function of the number of evaluations $n$, over
five paired repetitions (the $8$ initial random points are identical between
the two arms). The effect of the prior is immediate: the \emph{first}
model-suggested point already lands at the global optimum, and after one
Bayesian batch ($n = 12$) every proxy-informed run sits within $0.008$ of the
optimum --- a relative optimality gap of $0.2\%$, i.e.\ at the scale of the
proxy's own error band --- while the across-repetition spread collapses from
$\pm 0.45$ to $\pm 0.001$. The flat prior, by contrast, is still climbing at
the end of the budget and terminates $0.59$ below the optimum on average (an
$18\%$ gap) with nearly seventy times the spread. Reading the comparison in
terms of evaluations rather than attained value: the proxy-informed optimiser
needs $12$ evaluations to surpass the level the flat prior has not reached
after $24$. For an objective costing months per evaluation, this is the
difference between one and several years of computation. The trained
amplitude factor is doing its intended work: across repetitions it settled at
$c \in [0.69, 0.89]$, i.e.\ the marginal likelihood tightened the band
$10$--$30\%$ below the worst-case bound without ever being allowed to exceed
it. We note the benchmark is deliberately favourable --- the proxy is
everywhere within $1\%$ of the truth and the distortion is smooth --- but
this is precisely the regime the method is designed for, and the guarantee
that makes it favourable is one the user must certify, not one the method
assumes silently.

\section{Limitations and future work}

\paragraph{Soft bound.} The hard guarantee \eqref{eq:bound} is encoded as a
$\kappa$-sigma Gaussian band; at $\kappa = 2$, roughly $5\%$ of prior mass
violates the bound pointwise. Truncated or constrained GPs would encode the
bound exactly at considerably greater inferential cost.

\paragraph{Calibration of the residual smoothness.} The bound fixes the
\emph{amplitude} of the residual but not its \emph{lengthscale}; the latter is
learned from data as usual. A proxy whose error oscillates faster than the
learned lengthscale will be modelled overconfidently between evaluations.

\paragraph{Zero-data suggestion.} With an informative prior the surrogate is
meaningful before any expensive evaluation, so in principle the initial random
phase could be replaced by acquisition over the prior alone. The current
implementation still requires one batch of evaluations before the model is
activated (its normalisers need at least two points); removing this
restriction is mechanical but not yet done.

\paragraph{Gradients and memoisation.} The proxy is currently evaluated
without gradients --- harmless under derivative-free acquisition optimisation,
but a gradient-based acquisition optimiser would require differentiating
through the prior mean. Memoisation of proxy calls would make second-scale
proxies practical.

\section{Conclusion}

When an expensive objective comes with a cheap surrogate and a quantitative
pointwise error guarantee --- the everyday situation in computational
chemistry and many other simulation sciences --- that guarantee can be
installed directly into the Gaussian process prior of a Bayesian optimiser:
proxy as prior mean, error band as (heteroscedastic) prior standard deviation,
bound as a hard cap on a trained amplitude. The construction is exact in its
bookkeeping (valid PSD covariance, correct behaviour under data whitening),
composes with standard kernels and acquisition machinery, and is implemented
in \veropt{} in one self-contained module plus a handful of local changes,
documented here alongside a runnable example. In benchmark experiments it
converts a months-per-point optimisation that would otherwise spend its
budget rediscovering known structure into one that starts at the proxy and
spends every evaluation on the residual.
